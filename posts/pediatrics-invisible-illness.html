Invisible Illness: The Hidden Cause Behind Rising Teenage ‘Laziness’

Across the world, teens rise in the morning for school, balance social and academic expectations, only to end their days with less-than-optimal amounts of sleep; however, some go about their day sleeping at every possible moment. On the way to school, at school, after school, and even during activities, they actually want to do. They get labeled ‘lazy’ and ‘unmotivated’, often by adults who jump to the most typical teenage stereotypes because they fail to question the ‘why’.

Drawing the Line between Teenage Laziness and Illness

Teens are known for their resilience, so when their bodies are constantly deprived of what they should have in abundance, it begs the question: how many ‘lazy’ adolescents are actually unwell?

Recent research from Columbia University Mailman School of Public Health shows that mental health concerns among young people have been steadily increasing worldwide, particularly among adolescent girls. Many of these struggles are “internalizing,” meaning they appear through exhaustion, withdrawal, low motivation, or sadness rather than obvious disruptive behavior.

Adolescence is a period marked by a staggering amount of changes. Hormones, brain development, physical growth, and teenagers are constantly in a state of limbo, allowing concerning symptoms to be written off as ‘temporary developmental phenomena’ or, better yet, due to ‘heightened age-associated anxiety’.

Despite my earlier points, it’s not just physical issues that are increasing globally in our youth. Mental health concerns like anxiety, depression, and psychological distress are rising, impacting the overall wellness of young girls. Unlike many physical conditions, mental health concerns always seem to be well known by all doctors. More accurately, one specific condition. Regardless of the symptoms many teenage girls display, many receive the same diagnosis of anxiety, subjecting them to years of medical neglect and gaslighting.

Though I wonder, if teens are simply supposed to ‘bounce back’ no matter the ailment, why can’t adults recognize the severity of situations they don’t?

Defining Invisible Illness

You may have never heard the term ‘Invisible Illness’ or seen one. Why? It’s in the name. Unless you know what you’re looking for, you’d likely never spot them. This term isn’t for a specific few conditions, rather all medical issues that aren’t visible, making them more obvious than situations like missing a limb or cancer, hair loss.

Invisible Illnesses can include both physical and mental health conditions, ranging from chronic fatigue and autonomic disorders to anxiety and depression. According to Harvard Health Publishing, these conditions may not show outward physical signs, yet they can still severely interfere with daily functioning, energy levels, concentration, and emotional well-being. Because symptoms are ‘hidden’, many people living with these conditions are dismissed by society and even their own doctors with phrases like “you look fine” even when they are struggling internally. This creates a paradox where, for many adolescents, the hardest part of an invisible illness isn’t the symptoms themselves, but convincing others that those symptoms are real.

Symptoms Mistaken for “Laziness”

When most adults see a teenager sleeping in class, they quickly assume they either stayed up all night on something like video games, are disinterested, or are simply lazy. This stereotype, even if they are often correct, does more harm than good. Here are common invisible illnesses that parallel teenage stereotypes:

Adults See: Sleeping all day — Possible Reality: Chronic fatigue

Adults See: Missing school — Possible Reality: POTS symptom

Adults See: Poor concentration — Possible Reality: Brain fog

Adults See: Lack of motivation — Possible Reality: Depression

Adults See: Isolation — Possible Reality: Anxiety/exhaustion

Studies surrounding adolescent fatigue and common conditions such as POTS (Postural Orthostatic Tachycardia Syndrome) show that symptoms like dizziness, headaches, nausea, brain fog, and exhaustion may seriously disrupt school performance and social life. Yet, because affected teens may still look outwardly ‘healthy’, their symptoms are frequently minimized or misunderstood.

The Damage of Being Called Lazy

Although on one hand, being labeled as ‘lazy’ affects the quality of health care a teen may receive for years to come, it also damages them emotionally. All of their issues being swept under a, truthfully, insulting label, changes the way they act.

Being repeatedly told that their symptoms are exaggerated or imaginary can create shame, self-doubt, and emotional isolation, causing a harmful cycle of discomfort and depression. Some adolescents eventually stop advocating for themselves altogether, fearing they will once again be dismissed as dramatic, irresponsible, or the dreaded “lazy”.

Adolescence is key for building secure trust habits, so teaching a child to doubt themselves or even, you will affect them for life. Many teens go down two paths, with some intermixing in the middle. They may come to believe their ‘trusted adults’ don’t truly have their best interests in mind, or simply stop asking for help and start internalizing the label, pushing their body into irreparable damage.

If things do come to this, they’re often questioned as to why they stayed quiet. The uncomfortable teenage phenomenon of always being ‘at fault’.

Support Teen Struggles

Not every struggling teenager is lazy. Some are fighting physical illnesses no one can see, while others are carrying invisible psychological burdens that quietly chip away at their energy and motivation day by day. Before labeling adolescents as unmotivated or irresponsible, adults should consider digging deeper and making sure the teens who trust them are not fighting their battles alone.
